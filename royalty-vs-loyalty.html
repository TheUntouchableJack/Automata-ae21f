Royalty or loyalty? You probably mean loyalty — here's the difference

A royalty is money paid to an owner for the use of their work — a song, a book, a patent, a brand. A loyalty program rewards customers for coming back. If you run a shop, café, salon, gym, or clinic and you want your regulars to visit more often, the word you want is loyalty.

Why the mix-up happens so often

The two words are one letter apart, they sound nearly identical out loud, and both get used around money and customers. Type "royalty program for my coffee shop" into a search engine and every result you get back will be about loyalty programs — because that's what people mean when they search it. Search engines quietly correct the slip millions of times a day.

It's also a genuinely reasonable mistake. "Treat your customers like royalty" is a phrase most people have heard, so pairing royalty with customer rewards feels natural even though the actual business term is something else.

Royalty

A payment for using someone's property

Owner gets paid every time their work is used. Money flows to the creator or rights holder.

Loyalty

A reward for coming back

Customer earns something for repeat business. Value flows to the customer, to keep them returning.

What a royalty actually is

A royalty is a payment made to the owner of an asset in exchange for the right to use it. It's usually a percentage of revenue or a fixed fee per use, and it's a legal and accounting concept rather than a marketing one. You'll run into royalties in places like:

  • Music. Songwriters, performers and publishers earn royalties every time a track is streamed, sold, played on the radio, or used in a film.
  • Books and publishing. An author typically earns a percentage of each copy sold.
  • Patents and licensing. An inventor licenses a patent to a manufacturer and is paid a royalty per unit made or sold.
  • Franchising. A franchisee pays the franchisor an ongoing royalty — often a percentage of gross sales — for the right to operate under the brand.
  • Natural resources. A landowner is paid a royalty on oil, gas, or minerals extracted from their land.

What a loyalty program actually is

A loyalty program is a system for rewarding customers who come back. The mechanics are familiar even if the name isn't: the punch card at the coffee shop, the points balance at the pharmacy, the tiers at an airline. All of them do the same job — make the next visit slightly more attractive than it would otherwise have been.

The usual building blocks are:

  • Earning. Customers accumulate points, stamps, or visits. Some programs count spend; others simply count how often someone shows up.
  • Rewards. Those points get redeemed for something worth having — a free coffee, a discount, an upgrade.
  • Tiers. Regulars who visit most often unlock better treatment, which gives occasional customers a reason to become regulars.
  • Follow-up. The part most small businesses skip: reaching back out to a customer who hasn't been in for a while, or wishing them a happy birthday with something attached.

The reason any of it works is arithmetic. Winning a brand-new customer costs money — ads, discounts, time. Getting an existing happy customer to come back one extra time a month costs almost nothing by comparison. A loyalty program is just a structured way of making that second thing happen on purpose instead of by luck.
